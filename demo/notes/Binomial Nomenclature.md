# Binomial Nomenclature

Binomial nomenclature is the standardized system for naming species using two Latin or Latinized
words: the genus name followed by a specific epithet. It was formalized by Carl Linnaeus in the 18th
century and remains the foundation of modern biological naming, as outlined in the
[[Taxonomy Overview]].

The first part of the name identifies the genus, always capitalized, such as "Panthera" in
[[Genus Panthera]]. The second part, the species epithet, is lowercase and describes the particular
species — for example, "tigris" in Panthera tigris, the [[Species Panthera Tigris]]. Both words are
conventionally italicized in print.

This two-part system avoids the ambiguity of common names, which vary between languages and regions.
A single species may have dozens of local names, but its binomial name is recognized by scientists
worldwide.

Occasionally a third term is added for subspecies, forming a trinomial name. Binomial nomenclature
also applies beyond animals, covering plants, fungi, and microorganisms, making it one of the most
universal conventions in the biological sciences.
